Who defines the objective?

An agent can follow its instructions correctly and still produce an unfair result. My marking agent is the case, and the thread tested where human oversight has to sit.

About this discussion

About this page: Collaborative Learning Discussion 1, Units 1 to 3. Only my own posts appear. Peers’ posts are not published, and where my posts named a peer, the name is replaced with a description. A short line in italics gives the context for each reply.

Discussion topic: Agent Based Systems. Discuss what has led to the rise of agent-based systems and the benefits that this approach can offer to organisations.

Initial post

Posted 27 October 2026.

The rise of agent-based systems has been driven by increasingly interconnected, distributed and complex computing environments. These environments require systems that can respond to change without constant human direction. Intelligent agents offer reactivity, proactiveness and social ability, allowing organisations to coordinate activity, make faster decisions and reduce routine supervision (Wooldridge and Jennings, 1995; Wooldridge, 2009).

What interests me is the idea of a “delegated objective”. Wooldridge (2009) explains that an agent acts autonomously to achieve goals assigned by an owner or user. Its autonomy is mainly over how it acts, while someone else still decides what success means. This makes the question of whose interests an agent serves part of its design.

I can see this in a local marking agent I am slowly developing for OneNote. It completes a first review of student work against my rubric and flags categories that may require closer attention before I assign the final mark. This saves me time, but I still decide what the rubric values. If my criteria reward easily detected completion rather than genuine understanding, the agent could apply them consistently without supporting student learning. The time saved is mine, but if the criteria are wrong, the cost falls on students who had no say in setting them. This is related to the specification problems discussed by Amodei et al. (2016), including reward hacking, where success against a measurable objective can differ from the outcome actually intended.

Agent-based systems offer real organisational benefits, but the benefits and the risks may fall on different people. The key question is therefore not only whether an agent can act independently, but who defines its objective and who bears the consequences.

References

  • Amodei, D., Olah, C., Steinhardt, J., Christiano, P., Schulman, J. and Mané, D. (2016) ‘Concrete problems in AI safety’, arXiv, 1606.06565. doi: 10.48550/arXiv.1606.06565.
  • Wooldridge, M. (2009) An Introduction to MultiAgent Systems. 2nd edn. Chichester: John Wiley & Sons.
  • Wooldridge, M. and Jennings, N.R. (1995) ‘Intelligent agents: theory and practice’, The Knowledge Engineering Review, 10(2), pp. 115–152. doi: 10.1017/S0269888900008122.

Reply in my own thread

Posted 30 October 2026.

A peer replying to my initial post connected it to value sensitive design and asked whether the agent’s flags had ever disagreed with my own judgement.

I have not yet seen a direct disagreement between the agent’s flags and my final judgement, mainly because the system is deliberately limited to a first-line review. Its most successful feature is the summary table it creates for each class and lesson. It reviews only the students who have submitted work through Teams, which also makes it easier to identify who has and has not submitted. However, it does not assign the final grade.

The more revealing mismatch has been between the efficiency I expected and the system’s actual performance. The first batch took longer than marking by hand because of the initial setup. My home PC has access to OneNote but cannot run the local model, while my Mac can run the model. I now maintain a handoff document and backup copies of the folders so that later batches can start more efficiently.

Edge cases have also required human judgement. Some students submitted only a PNG rather than a PDF. I decided to assess the visible work instead of treating compliance with a particular file format as evidence of learning. This reinforced your point about value-sensitive design. The issue is not simply whether the agent applies the rubric consistently, but whether the workflow reflects values that are fair to the students affected by it (Friedman and Hendry, 2019).

Your suggestion about student input is important, and it is something I already build into my teaching. I ask students whether the rubric reflects what they learned and use their feedback to refine both the assessment and the lesson. This could also become a useful check on the marking agent because students may identify aspects of learning that are meaningful but not easily detected from the submitted work. Their input informs the process, although responsibility for aligning the rubric with the course outcomes remains with me.

My next step may be a multi-agent version in which a Mac mini coordinates tasks and switches models between batches. I am still considering how to make the system more agentic without weakening its ethical safeguards. Wooldridge (2009) distinguishes an agent’s autonomy over how it acts from the objectives delegated to it. Following that distinction, the agents could organise evidence, provisionally apply rubric criteria and flag uncertainty, but they would not assign or change a final mark. I would also want every flag to be traceable to visible evidence. This would make the system more operationally autonomous while keeping assessment authority and responsibility with me.

References

  • Friedman, B. and Hendry, D.G. (2019) Value Sensitive Design: Shaping Technology with Moral Imagination. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press.
  • Wooldridge, M. (2009) An Introduction to MultiAgent Systems. 2nd edn. Chichester: John Wiley & Sons.

Response to a peer: network monitoring

Posted 27 October 2026.

Written on a peer’s initial post about automated network monitoring and adjustable autonomy in broadcasting.

I can relate to your cautious approach. As mentioned in my initial post, I am gradually developing a local marking agent that performs a first review before I make the final decision. Your network example helped me see two limitations in that approach: the person approving may come to rely too heavily on the agent, and approval does not correct the objective the agent was given.

Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens (2000) divide automation into four stages: information acquisition, information analysis, decision and action selection, and action implementation. Your proposal, where the agent suggests an action and an engineer approves it, automates up to decision and action selection while keeping implementation human. They also stress that automation changes the human role, often in ways designers did not intend. This matters because retaining final authority may provide less protection if the person begins to depend on the automated recommendation. Onnasch et al. (2014) similarly found that higher levels of automation can improve routine performance but reduce situation awareness when the automation fails.

The second limitation concerns the objective itself. Wooldridge (2009) describes agents as acting towards delegated objectives, but engineers still decide which conditions the system treats as abnormal and what response it recommends. A monitoring agent could apply its rules correctly while still recommending that important traffic be blocked. Human approval limits the agent’s authority, but it does not necessarily correct the thresholds or priorities built into its objective.

I agree that adjustable autonomy is necessary, especially in broadcasting. In your network, who sets the monitoring thresholds, and would the viewers or broadcast clients affected by a blocked channel have any input into what the system treats as acceptable risk?

References

  • Onnasch, L., Wickens, C.D., Li, H. and Manzey, D. (2014) ‘Human performance consequences of stages and levels of automation: an integrated meta-analysis’, Human Factors, 56(3), pp. 476–488.
  • Parasuraman, R., Sheridan, T.B. and Wickens, C.D. (2000) ‘A model for types and levels of human interaction with automation’, IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics – Part A: Systems and Humans, 30(3), pp. 286–297.
  • Wooldridge, M. (2009) An Introduction to MultiAgent Systems. 2nd edn. Chichester: John Wiley & Sons.

Response to a peer: AI in business

Posted 8 November 2026.

Written on a peer’s initial post about AI and business productivity, after another peer had separated AI in general from agent-based systems.

I agree with another peer’s point that there is an important difference between AI in general and agents that are given some autonomy. I was also interested in your point about employee skills development. The benefits of AI depend not only on what the technology can do, but also on whether employees know how to work with it and can recognize when human judgement is still needed.

I have often said that, when used properly, AI can help us become a version 2.0 of ourselves. It can help us reach goals or develop abilities that we may not have thought were possible before. Dellermann et al. (2019) describe this as hybrid intelligence, where people and AI combine their different strengths to achieve more than either could achieve alone. AI should not replace human intelligence and creativity. It should help extend them.

Automating repetitive tasks may improve efficiency, but an organization should not measure success only by how much work is completed or how much time is saved. It should also consider the accuracy of the output, the quality of the decisions, and whether employees remain confident enough to question the system rather than simply accepting its recommendations.

References

  • Dellermann, D., Ebel, P., Söllner, M. and Leimeister, J.M. (2019) ‘Hybrid Intelligence’, Business & Information Systems Engineering, 61(5), pp. 637–643. doi: 10.1007/s12599-019-00595-2.

Summary post

Posted 8 November 2026.

In my initial post, I argued that an agent can follow its instructions correctly and still produce an unfair result. My marking agent conducts a first review of student work, but I decide what the rubric values and assign the final mark. That boundary matters because a weak rubric could reward what is easy to detect rather than what students have actually learned.

One peer’s network example showed how closing alerts quickly can appear efficient while the underlying faults remain unresolved. Russell and Norvig (2021) illustrate the same problem through a vacuum-cleaner agent that satisfies its performance measure while missing its real purpose. Another peer helped me connect this distinction to my own system. Processing every submission says little about whether the assessment remains fair and educationally meaningful.

Some students submitted a PNG instead of the requested PDF. I assessed the visible work because the file format was not evidence of learning. The first peer’s point about value-sensitive design helped me look beyond consistent rule-following. Friedman and Hendry (2019) frame value-sensitive design as designing around the values of everyone a system affects, direct and indirect stakeholders alike. The agent’s evidence rules must reflect the needs of students as well as the efficiency needs of the teacher.

Onnasch et al. (2014) found that the more stages of a task designers automated, the worse operators performed when the automation failed. Keeping final authority with a person provides one safeguard. Oversight must also include regular review of the rubric, thresholds and objectives shaping the agent’s recommendations.

I still believe that AI, used properly, can help us become a version 2.0 of ourselves. That depends on traceable evidence and human judgement. My agent can organize evidence, apply criteria provisionally and flag uncertainty. Final assessment authority and responsibility remain with me.

References

  • Friedman, B. and Hendry, D.G. (2019) Value Sensitive Design: Shaping Technology with Moral Imagination. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press.
  • Onnasch, L., Wickens, C.D., Li, H. and Manzey, D. (2014) ‘Human performance consequences of stages and levels of automation: an integrated meta-analysis’, Human Factors, 56(3), pp. 476–488.
  • Russell, S. and Norvig, P. (2021) Artificial Intelligence: A Modern Approach. 4th edn, Global edn. Harlow: Pearson.
