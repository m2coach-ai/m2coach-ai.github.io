AI: Balancing Innovation with Security and Ethical Considerations

Recording

Introduction

Across every sector, including healthcare, finance, education, and law, organisations are deploying AI faster than they can govern it. The technology moves. The accountability structures do not. That gap between what AI promises and what it costs is what this presentation is about.

Why AI, and Why Now

I chose to focus on AI because of how quickly it has moved from a specialist technology into something shaping everyday life, business, and education.

Since ChatGPT’s public release, the use, discussion, and economic impact of AI have grown exponentially, but governance has not kept pace. To frame that, I draw on Floridi and Cowls, who propose five principles for ethical AI. The first four—beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, and justice—come from bioethics. Their fifth principle, explicability, is the key one for this presentation. It combines intelligibility, meaning how a system works, with accountability, meaning who is responsible when it fails. Their argument is that without explicability, the other principles cannot really be enforced. A system that cannot explain its decisions cannot be held to any meaningful standard at all (Floridi and Cowls, 2019).

Floridi and colleagues extend that argument in AI4People through what they call the dual advantage of ethics: good governance does not just prevent harm, it also helps organisations identify opportunities and avoid costly mistakes. In that sense, ethics is not a brake on innovation. It is part of what makes innovation sustainable (Floridi et al., 2018).

So my argument is this: enterprise AI cannot be governed by principles alone. Without enforceable accountability structures, ethical frameworks remain aspirational, and the gap between what AI promises and what it costs will keep widening (Floridi and Cowls, 2019; Floridi et al., 2018).

What AI Offers Enterprises

The business case for AI is well established.

Research from MIT Technology Review found that 82 per cent of senior executives see scaling AI as a top priority for creating business value (MIT Technology Review Insights, 2024).

A study by Dell’Acqua and colleagues found performance improvements of nearly 40 per cent when highly skilled knowledge workers used generative AI within its capability boundary (Dell’Acqua et al., 2023).

PwC estimates that AI could contribute up to 15.7 trillion dollars to the global economy by 2030. For enterprises, those numbers are extremely persuasive (Rao and Verweij, 2017).

But the speed of adoption is where the problem begins. NIST identifies four domains of AI risk: governance, mapping, measurement, and management. Each one requires its own processes, expertise, and institutional commitment. The 2024 Generative AI Profile extends that framework by identifying additional risks specific to large language models, including hallucination, privacy violations, security risks, and the generation of harmful content. The pattern across both frameworks is consistent: organisations are deploying AI faster than they can govern, measure, or manage it (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023; National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2024). That asymmetry is what the rest of this presentation examines.

The Ethical Cost

The ethical costs of that gap show up most clearly in three areas.

The first is bias. Mittelstadt and colleagues identify six ethical concerns with algorithmic systems, and the first three inconclusive evidence, inscrutable evidence, and misguided evidence point to the same structural problem: algorithms reproduce the assumptions frozen into their training data. Unfair outcomes are not simply bugs. They are often outputs of the system itself (Mittelstadt et al., 2016).

A common response is to treat this as an engineering problem: better data, more diverse training sets, and improved testing. But Mittelstadt and colleagues reject that framing. Solving the technical problem does not automatically solve the moral one, because the decision to deploy a system that has not been audited for fairness is itself an ethical failure, not just a technical oversight (Mittelstadt et al., 2016).

The second issue is opacity. Floridi and Cowls position explicability as the enabling principle for a reason. If a system cannot explain how it reached a decision, there is no meaningful way to challenge it and no clear basis for holding anyone responsible (Floridi and Cowls, 2019).

The third issue is the accountability gap itself. Responsibility becomes dispersed across developers, deployers, and users. When no single party is clearly accountable, harm goes uncorrected (Mittelstadt et al., 2016).

The Security Cost

The security risks are just as concrete. Pelekis and colleagues provide a useful taxonomy here. They identify three major forms of adversarial attack: evasion attacks that fool a model at the point of use, poisoning attacks that corrupt it during training, and privacy attacks that extract what a model knows. Their central finding is sobering: there is no universal defence that works against all attack types. Trade-offs are unavoidable. Robustness can reduce accuracy, privacy can reduce utility, and stronger defences can reduce practicality (Pelekis et al., 2025).

Deepfakes sit at the intersection of security risk and social harm. The issue is not novelty it is scale. Visual and voice-based signals that have historically been treated as reliable can now be very convincingly fabricated. I will come back to this in more depth shortly.

Achuthan and colleagues reach a similar conclusion from another angle. AI is both shield and attack surface. The same qualities that make it useful for defence: pattern recognition, anomaly detection, and behavioural analysis, also make it highly useful for attackers. In other words, AI is accelerating both sides of the cybersecurity arms race (Achuthan et al., 2024).

The deepfake problem makes that escalation visible. Industry reporting from Signicat suggests that deepfake fraud attempts in the financial sector have risen by more than two thousand per cent over the last three years, and that a large share of detected fraud attempts are now AI-driven (Signicat, 2025). The Arup case illustrates the practical cost: in early 2024, the company lost around twenty-five million US dollars after an employee was deceived during a deepfake video call impersonating senior colleagues (World Economic Forum, 2025). The point is not simply that deepfakes are increasing. It is that enterprises are now operating in an environment where familiar visual and verbal signals can no longer be treated as reliable forms of trust.

The Cost of Governance Failure: Two Cases

These two cases make the cost of governance failure visible in practice.

The Air Canada case illustrates a different kind of failure: not safety, but accountability. In Moffatt v. Air Canada, the British Columbia Civil Resolution Tribunal ruled that the airline was liable for incorrect bereavement fare advice given by its website chatbot. A customer booked full-fare tickets after being told a discount could be applied retroactively within ninety days. It could not (Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024).

Air Canada’s defence is worth pausing on. The company attempted to distance itself from the chatbot’s output, arguing that the tool operated independently and should bear its own legal responsibility. The tribunal dismissed that outright. The ruling was clear: an organisation is accountable for the information provided through its platform, whether that information comes from a human being or from an automated system (Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024).

The financial damages were relatively small—about six hundred and fifty Canadian dollars. But the reputational damage was far larger, with international coverage following the case. Air Canada’s failure was not technological. It was organisational. Enterprises cannot deploy AI and then disclaim responsibility for what it produces (Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024).

The Tumbler Ridge case makes the cost of that environment deeply personal. Based on CBC reporting, in February 2026, eight people were killed at Tumbler Ridge Secondary School in British Columbia: five students, an educational assistant, and two family members. The shooter then took her own life (CBC News, 2026a).

What emerged afterwards was this. OpenAI had banned the shooter’s ChatGPT account in June 2025 after posts indicating a potential risk of gun violence were flagged internally. Employees requested escalation to leadership and recommended informing Canadian law enforcement. OpenAI decided that the activity did not meet its threshold for police reporting. The shooter later created a second account, bypassed the ban, and carried out the attack. OpenAI has since admitted that, under its enhanced protocol, the account would be referred to law enforcement if discovered today (CBC News, 2026a; CBC News, 2026b).

When this story broke, I was silent. School safety protocols have been part of my professional reality for years. Entry screening, procedures for visitors, all of that. But those protocols would not have stopped something like this. It is a stark reminder that a place designed to be safe for students can also be the place where vulnerability is most concentrated.

Analytically, what Tumbler Ridge exposed was not a failure of detection. It was a failure of escalation. The monitoring existed. The obligation to act on it did not. Canada has no federal AI regulatory framework requiring companies like OpenAI to report potentially violent users to law enforcement. That absence is not a gap in technology. It is a gap in governance (CBC News, 2026b).

Taken together, these cases show that AI risk becomes organisational risk when accountability structures are weak or absent.

Regulatory and Governance Frameworks

Two governance frameworks are trying to close this gap, but they come from very different directions.

The first is the EU AI Act, passed in 2024, which is the first binding AI legislation globally. It classifies systems by risk tier—unacceptable, high, limited, and minimal—prohibits certain applications outright, and mandates transparency obligations for high-risk systems. Its reach also extends beyond Europe, applying to systems that affect EU citizens regardless of where the developer is based (European Parliament, 2024).

The second framework is the NIST AI Risk Management Framework. It takes a very different approach. NIST is voluntary, not regulatory. It is designed to plug into existing enterprise risk processes through four functions: govern, map, measure, and manage. The 2024 GenAI Profile extends this guidance to large language models, identifying additional risks without prescribing specific controls (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023; National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2024).

That flexibility is also its limitation. NIST provides structure and language, but it cannot compel organisations to act. And Canada, as Tumbler Ridge made visible, currently has neither at the federal level (CBC News, 2026b).

Balancing Innovation with Ethics

So how do enterprises actually balance innovation with ethics?

The answer, I would argue, is structural. Floridi and colleagues frame ethics as a strategic function rather than a compliance burden. The dual advantage means that good governance not only prevents costly failures, but also helps organisations identify opportunities that careless deployment would miss (Floridi et al., 2018).

NIST provides the operational architecture for that through its four core functions, with governance sitting above the other three as the accountability layer that makes them meaningful in practice (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023).

A current example of this came from OpenAI, which shelved a planned feature after its wellness advisory council unanimously warned against launch. At the same time, the company was facing multiple lawsuits alleging that ChatGPT had contributed to user deaths. What matters here is not just the feature itself, but the decision-making logic behind it: a company had built something, faced governance concerns, and chose not to release it. That is the dual advantage in action. Ethics functioning as strategy, not as an afterthought.

Conclusion

Principles alone do not prevent harm. The cases I have discussed show what happens when AI is monitored without an obligation to act, or deployed without clear responsibility for its outputs (CBC News, 2026a; Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024).

Ethical frameworks and voluntary standards offer guidance, but guidance without enforcement leaves accountability optional. When accountability is optional, it is often deferred (Floridi and Cowls, 2019; National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023).

So the conclusion is this: enforceable accountability structures, not aspirational guidelines, are what close the gap between AI innovation and its costs. The enterprises that build governance in from the start are the ones that will still be trusted when failure is no longer hypothetical (Floridi et al., 2018; European Parliament, 2024).
