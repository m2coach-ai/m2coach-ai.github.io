Agentic AI blog post

A blog post for a general reader on agentic AI, using the EchoLeak vulnerability and a Zapier case.

The post

In June 2025, Microsoft quietly patched a vulnerability called EchoLeak. Researchers at Aim Labs had found that a single crafted email, one that never had to be opened or clicked, could quietly tell Microsoft 365 Copilot to send a user’s private files back to the attacker (Reddy and Gujral, 2025). It was the first documented zero-click exploit against an AI agent. The significance is not the patch. It is the category.

Here is the more interesting version of the same category shift. At Zapier, an engineer hooked Claude Cowork up to the company’s internal systems and asked it to find operational bottlenecks in engineering. The agent ran fifteen SQL queries across six tools, pulled the results together, and came back with a dashboard that showed where work was getting stuck and which teams were feeling it (Anthropic, 2026). That would have taken an analyst weeks to build by hand. None of it is content generation. It is research and coordination work, done across multiple tools, with real judgement calls along the way. Adabara et al. (2025) describe this as the defining feature of agentic AI: systems that pursue goals proactively rather than waiting to be prompted, executing workflows across environments with minimal human oversight. That shift is already reshaping what knowledge work looks like in practice.

The productivity evidence on this is strong and unusually specific. Dell’Acqua et al. (2026) put 758 Boston Consulting Group consultants through a controlled study, and the ones using GPT-4 on tasks inside the AI’s capability frontier completed roughly 12% more work, finished 25% faster, and produced output rated over 40% higher in quality than their colleagues working unaided. Those are not marketing numbers. They are peer-reviewed findings from Organization Science, and they describe a chat-era tool. Agentic systems such as Anthropic’s Claude Cowork, released in January 2026 and now available across all paid tiers, extend that same capability to multi-step tasks that were previously too fiddly to delegate: reading a folder of receipts and building an expense report, compiling a research brief, turning meeting transcripts into follow-up actions.

The risks are real, and they are the reason governance cannot be an afterthought. But the harder, more honest question for most enterprises is not whether to adopt agentic AI. It is which parts of knowledge work still deserve a human’s full attention, and which ones we have been spending that attention on only because nothing else could do them. That is not a threat to the profession. It is a rare invitation to decide what the profession is actually for.
