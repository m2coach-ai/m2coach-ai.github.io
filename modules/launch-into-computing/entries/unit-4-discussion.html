Unit 4 discussion: AI in education

The initial post, a reply and the summary post from one Collaborative Learning Discussion. Only my own text appears. Peers’ replies are not published.

Initial post

28 January 2026

I would like to focus my part of the discussion on artificial intelligence in education because it plays out in front of me every day in the classroom. I’ve seen it become a dominant tool for students. AI is used to bridge gaps that previously slowed their learning. I’ve watched English language learners translate complex instructions into their home languages within seconds, and students who hesitate with abstract concepts ask AI to rephrase ideas in ways that finally make sense to them. Sometimes what slows students down is simply needing the steps broken down clearly, and AI can do that well. Some of my learners who struggle with processing or are still building basic literacy skills have started using AI as their go‑to support.

The shift is not limited to students. Teachers are looking for ways to use AI as well. Our provincial government has even proposed its use to help address teacher workload. Colleagues use it to analyse lesson plans, adjust activities for accommodations, and experiment with generating new ideas for classroom engagement. As teachers, we’re learning in real time how to integrate these tools without losing the human judgment that makes teaching work. I am curious about others who are teachers here and how they are using AI. Also, those that are not educators the use of AI in this fashion seen as a positive or are there reservations that you have?

Still, I can’t ignore the other side of what I see. Some students use AI to produce answers without developing any underlying understanding. They want to receive praise for work that isn’t their own, even though they feel it is. They’ve traded the eureka moment for instant gratification without any struggle. Reading Amodei’s (2024) description of AI as a technology in its “adolescence” resonated with me; the behaviour I see in classrooms mirrors that metaphor. The tools are powerful, and they’re growing stronger, but our maturity in how we use that strength hasn’t caught up.

Reference

  • Amodei, D. (2024) The adolescence of technology. Available at: https://darioamodei.com/essay/the-adolescence-of-technology (Accessed: 27 January 2026).

My response to a peer

10 February 2026. A peer asked how I, as an educator, might improve retention of information for students learning with an AI assistant.

I will directly address your inquiry from my personal experience. I have implemented an inquiry-based model with the use of AI. Think of it as going down the rabbit hole. Students will follow up a question given to AI, and the subsequent answer, with at least two other questions. This is supporting the students’ natural sense of inquiry, aligning with the framework for “educative AI” proposed by Su and Yang (2023). A second technique that I have attempted to implement is an analysis of the answer provided, then teaching of the concept to myself or to another student so they can replicate the solution. This utilizes the “Protégé Effect”, where the act of preparing to teach information has been shown to significantly enhance conceptual learning and long-term retention (Fiorella and Mayer, 2013).

My goal is for students to see how they can reach their full potential with the use of AI, and perhaps without realization, they are learning in a different fashion that is tapping into their curiosity. Do you think shifting the focus to this kind of active questioning would do enough to support the students’ ability to think critically and retain information?

References

  • Fiorella, L. and Mayer, R.E. (2013) ‘The relative benefits of learning by teaching and teaching expectancy’, Contemporary Educational Psychology, 38(4), pp. 281–288. Available at: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2013.06.001.
  • Su, J. and Yang, W. (2023) ‘Unlocking the Power of ChatGPT: A Framework for Applying Generative AI in Education’, ECNU Review of Education, 6(3), pp. 355–366. Available at: https://doi.org/10.1177/20965311231168423.

Summary post

Reading my initial thread, what stayed with me was how quickly I shifted the conversation from enthusiasm about AI in education to examining what students gain and quietly lose when these tools become their default. I see students using AI every day to translate, rephrase, and break down ideas, and while that still feels helpful, I was already uneasy about the long term effects. The responses, especially the first peer reply, pushed that further, and I agree with its argument. Its point about cognitive offloading did not just align with my concern, it sharpened it. Its use of desirable difficulties showed me that those small eureka moments are the encoding process that shortcuts remove (Bjork and Bjork, 2011).

The second peer reply’s point about cognitive debt pushed that further. I agree with that caution here. It made me rethink how easily students drift into dependency without noticing it (Kosmyna et al., 2025). My reply about inquiry chains and the Protege Effect now feels more like a counterweight than a solution. A counter view is that AI just shifts the work around, but the patterns suggest something else. And because the cognitive debt study happened in a controlled setting, it's unclear how far those findings stretch in real classrooms.

A third peer’s algorithm analysis example tied everything together. That reply described using AI as a learning partner rather than a shortcut, showing how it can support design processes that offer genuinely new learning experiences. I keep coming back to a simple idea. The structure you start with shapes what becomes visible later. It could be a truth table or algorithm, it doesn’t matter, but it sets the tone and can obscure alternative routes that were right there. Su and Yang's 2023 work on cognitive offloading connects directly with what Fiorella and Mayer found in 2013 about generative learning (Su and Yang, 2023; Fiorella and Mayer, 2013). The real challenge seems to be designing metacognitive scaffolding deliberately rather than hoping it shows up on its own.
