From Output To Judgment

Using Rolfe et al.'s (2001) What, So What, Now What structure, my central learning is that numerical analysis is not simply producing output. It is deciding whether the output deserves to be trusted. I entered with twenty years of teaching experience and a background in Alberta Mathematics 30-1, a senior high school course covering advanced functions, probability, vectors, and introductory calculus. The mathematical logic was often familiar, but I had never used R. Beginning R alongside a new school year felt disorienting and sometimes frustrating, especially when instructions assumed technical knowledge I did not yet have.

Learning R And Growing Confidence

I used module videos, additional videos, reference guides, and targeted AI support. AI helped me diagnose omissions and organize code in my early stages of learning, but I ran every command, checked the output, and retained responsibility for the statistical decisions. Reusing the COVID dataset with multiple projects gradually helped me to replace uncertainty with rhythm. I can now adapt and verify a script from a fresh R session, although I still use references for unfamiliar syntax, advanced diagnostics, and interaction models.

Tables And Plots Became Analytical Checks

Producing summary tables, contingency tables, and plots became part of how I checked an analysis. In R, I created descriptive tables with separate sample sizes, COVID frequency and percentage tables, and contingency tables comparing diabetes outcome with age and BMI categories. Inspecting expected counts showed that the Underweight BMI row contained values of 2.6 and 1.4, so I replaced the original chi-square analysis with a revised table and used Fisher's exact test as a check. Histograms exposed skew, boxplots showed overlap, and the blood-pressure scatter plot showed why a correlation coefficient was not the whole story. A polished plot could not repair weak data or an unsuitable model.

Brown Table II: Visualisation Became A Data Audit

I began the Brown (1994) activity as a visualisation task, but it became a data audit. Row 8’s counts totalled 490 while surrounding rows were near 545, and the printed percentages summed to only ninety percent. I reconstructed the neutral cell and marked it. On the discussion board, a peer suggested the shortfall might represent missing responses. I checked that against the published percentages and revised my reasoning. I had accepted AI-supplied subgroup figures before checking Brown’s article. Parasuraman and Manzey (2010) describe automation bias as accepting imperfect decision support, and that was my error. I cut the unverifiable figures and corrected the post publicly. AI now points me toward what is worth checking, but it is not evidence until I return to the source. The correction stays in the e-portfolio because revision is part of the work.

Where to see it

  • The peer’s alternative, tested against the printed percentages: Brown Table II: summary post

Feedback, Sharing, And Ongoing Preservation

The discussion boards became reciprocal rather than performative. Peers challenged the Brown reconstruction, extended my thinking about clustered observations, and showed how deleting outliers could change significance. I shared knowledge through peer replies and published AI-assisted learning guides that explain setup, code, expected output, interpretation, and common traps. Adrian's feedback encouraged this approach while suggesting shorter comments and printing full tables rather than only the first six rows. My e-portfolio is an ongoing record across the programme. It preserves questions, code, mistakes, feedback, decisions, and growth rather than serving only as a gallery of completed work.

The Largest Frustration: One Document, One Upload

The mathematics test was the largest frustration. My first emails argued fairness in frustrated language; I then rewrote them as a factual, procedural request, which is the register that earns a response. The transfer began while visible time remained, but the page became unresponsive and the document was not accepted. I received 76 out of 100 without the final question worth 20 marks, equivalent to ninety-five percent of the marks available on the accepted work. I felt panicked and shocked because the mathematical work existed. I now reserve submission time and capture confirmation, while still questioning a process that could not distinguish an on-time transfer from a late attempt.

Where to see it

  • The changed action: the submission protocols goal in the Professional Development Plan
  • The test and the emails: private, and not published here

P-Values, Confidence Intervals, And Bayesian Thinking

Understanding the null hypothesis was a lightbulb moment. A p-value compares observed evidence with what would be expected under a null model; it is not the probability that the null is true. Greenland et al. (2016) helped me recognise that p-values and confidence intervals depend on the whole model and its assumptions. My repeated COVID reports produced a tiny p-value, but failed independence created false precision. I qualified Tijssen's (2021) case for reporting intervals: an interval can become just another threshold if I use it only to check whether it crosses a null value. Bayesian analysis completed the conceptual loop by combining a prior and likelihood to form a posterior. That can be intuitive, but the prior still requires justification.

The Diabetes Capstone Tested Transfer

The final project tested whether I could transfer guided learning into an integrated analysis. I recoded clinically impossible zeros as missing, verified the change, and found that only 392 of 768 participants were complete across all variables. Cleaning raised mean blood pressure from 69.11 to 72.41. I compared deletion, imputation, and complete observations required for each analysis, then reported the actual sample size. When fitted values created a row mismatch, I traced the problem to omitted cases and used predict with newdata so unavailable predictions became missing. The final models reinforced trade-offs: a model containing seven predictors explained more variation but used half the sample, while 77 percent classification accuracy concealed sensitivity below sixty percent.

Outcomes, Skills, And Changed Actions

The module outcomes now connect clearly. I developed statistical understanding, applied methods to decisions, critically evaluated numerical interpretations, and communicated results through scripts, tables, plots, discussion, and video. The Skills Matrix shows my largest gains in critical thinking, digital work, communication, and ethical awareness. It also records a decline in interpersonal confidence because I missed seminars after the school year began. My Professional Development Plan turns reflection into action: reproducible scripts from a fresh session, source verification, Bayesian practice, safer submission protocols, and application to teaching. I have already used my R primer with students. Teaching R gives high school students a head start because they can turn large datasets into charts and explain what they show. The central change was moving from producing numerical output to judging whether that output deserved to be trusted.
