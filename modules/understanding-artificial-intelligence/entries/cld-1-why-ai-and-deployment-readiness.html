Collaborative Learning Discussion 1

An initial post on why AI investment surged and what readiness to deploy requires, a response to a peer on AI and authenticity, and the summary post that revises the opening position. Only my own text appears. Peers’ posts are not published.

Initial post

Kneusel (2023) asks the right question in the title of his second chapter: why now? The answer is convergence: data, hardware and algorithmic capacity arriving at scale simultaneously, around 2012. That convergence is what separates the current moment from the expert systems boom of the 1980s, which collapsed partly because the infrastructure was not there to support what the investment promised.

The healthcare sector makes this concrete. AI diagnostic systems have improved diagnostic accuracy and interpretation time in selected imaging tasks, although real world evidence remains more mixed (Lawrence et al., 2025). Faster and more consistent diagnosis can mean earlier intervention, fewer readmissions and reduced downstream expenditure. For a hospital trust or insurance provider, that is not a marginal gain. It is the kind of efficiency that can justify significant capital outlay.

Leslie (2019) identifies a core risk in public sector AI deployment: technical capability and responsible implementation are not the same thing. IBM Watson Health made this visible. The system was positioned as able to process clinical literature at a scale no human team could match. The investment logic was persuasive. It looked right. What hospitals eventually reported was that the recommendations did not translate reliably into clinical practice. IBM sold Watson Health assets to Francisco Partners in January 2022 (Reuters, 2022). The technology had not failed in a simple or obvious way. Rather, it produced outputs that looked authoritative enough to deploy before the organisational conditions to use them responsibly were in place.

Russell and Norvig (2021) identify this as the value alignment problem: the objectives built into a machine must actually reflect the outcomes humans need. For companies investing in AI, this is not a philosophical concern. It is operational. A system that optimises for the wrong metric, at scale, compounds the error with every decision it influences. Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the EU AI Act, reflects this concern at a regulatory level, with phased obligations for high risk AI systems around transparency, risk management and human oversight.

Investment in AI is not optional for organisations that intend to remain competitive. What that investment includes, especially whether governance and process design sit alongside the technology, is what determines whether the returns are real.

What examples have others seen where the deployment conditions were built before the technology arrived, rather than after?

Peer response

The active versus passive distinction is well made, but it leads somewhere the closing questions do not quite reach. If AI becomes the default across most consumer experiences, genuinely human made things do not become less valuable. They become rarer.

The wonky cake matters more when everything else looks the same. Frizzo et al. (2020) show that imperfection and naturalness can work as trust signals, particularly when consumers associate them with authenticity. Kirk and Givi (2025) also show measurable consumer distrust when AI authorship is detected. That reaction may grow as people become more alert to automated content, because the contrast between polished output and visible human effort will become easier to notice.

This sameness is not just an aesthetic problem. Doshi and Hauser (2024) found that AI tools can reduce collective diversity, meaning businesses drawing on the same models may start producing more similar content. For artisanal businesses, that is arguably good news. Their competitors polish up, converge, and start looking interchangeable.

A peer’s point about back office AI freeing up the owner's time is probably right for operational efficiency, though I am not sure the boundary between operational support and brand expression will stay as clean as it looks. Another peer’s menu translation example makes this boundary even messier, because the business may preserve its own authenticity while external AI systems reinterpret it for customers before they ever enter the shop.

Moravec et al. (2024) note that most people still do not fully register how much AI shapes what they consume. When that awareness catches up, the businesses that never had to perform authenticity because they simply had it may be in a stronger position than anyone anticipates.

So will authenticity lose its value? Probably the opposite.

Summary post

The thread divided more sharply than my initial post anticipated. Three peers accepted the deployment readiness framing and extended it through network engineering, BIM, and radiology. Two others both pushed against it, from different angles. For the first, the dilemma was sociological maturity and the fact that nobody was answerable when Watson failed. For the second, the convergence itself was incomplete, because ghost work and ecological cost sit underneath what looked like clean technical readiness. Both pushbacks land, and rereading the original post against them, I framed it too clean.

Peres et al. (2020) make this concrete in a way my initial post did not. Their systematic review found interpretability and cybersecurity were the design principles adopted least often in real Industrial AI deployments, and over seventy percent of deployments sit at autonomy Level 1, meaning humans still validate every meaningful decision. So the picture across sectors is not organisations gradually maturing toward responsible AI. It is deployment frozen at the level where the unresolved governance questions can be deferred. That reframes Watson Health. The hospitals were not unready for the technology. The accountability conditions for any AI to be trusted at that scale did not exist yet, and still do not.

Real accountability requires transparency about the decision logic itself, as Diakopoulos (2016) argues, not only the data feeding it. That was absent in Watson Health, and the absence was structural rather than incidental. The legal and organisational infrastructure to demand it remained underdeveloped, which is exactly Leslie's (2019) accountability gap. The first of them was right to name it.

So the position my initial post should have reached is this. Deployment readiness was the right diagnosis at the wrong scale. The conditions that matter are not organisational. They are structural within society, and that work had not been done.
